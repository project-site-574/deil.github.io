Depth Camera or RGB-to-Depth?
A Study of RGB-to-Depth Estimation for Robot Policy Learning

Anonymous RA-L Submission

Abstract

Point cloud–based robot policy learning has emerged as a powerful approach for robotic manipulation by enabling policies to directly exploit 3D geometric structure. In such pipelines, depth image acquisition plays a critical role in point cloud construction. However, raw depth from depth cameras is often noisy, sensitive to transparent, reflective, and repetitive surfaces, and limited by a minimum sensing distance. Recent advances in learned depth estimation offer a promising alternative: modern monocular, stereo, and multi-view approaches have achieved impressive performance in vision benchmarks. However, robotics still lacks a systematic understanding of how these advances translate to end-to-end policy learning and execution.

In this work, based on our introduced Depth-Estimated Imitation Learning (DEIL) general framework, we present the first systematic study of how modern depth estimation impacts point cloud–based imitation learning across two paradigms: In-Context Imitation Learning (ICIL) and Behaviour Cloning (BC). Within each paradigm, we keep the point cloud reconstruction procedure, policy backbone, and evaluation protocol fixed across depth sources. Through 71,800 simulated and 160 real-world rollouts, several depth estimators achieve reasonable downstream performance in simulation, although a consistent gap to GT depth remains. In real-world ICIL, the best-performing multi-view estimator is competitive with the tested RGB-D cameras on normal tasks and achieves higher success when depth sensing is unreliable. Our failure mode analysis further shows that reconstructed geometric quality generally correlates with policy success, with flying points being particularly harmful to BC. These findings highlight the need for improved geometric awareness and stability before learned depth estimation can be broadly deployed in robot manipulation.

DEIL: Depth-Estimated Imitation Learning

To systematically study the role of depth estimation in imitation learning, particularly under challenging perceptual conditions, we propose a general framework DEIL. The framework consists of three main components: (1) a depth estimation module, (2) a point cloud reconstruction module, and (3) an imitation learning module. This modular design allows us to plug in different depth estimation methods while holding the learning framework fixed, enabling controlled evaluation of their impact on robot policy performance.

First, in the depth estimation stage, raw RGB images are passed through a depth estimator to produce corresponding depth maps. Second, using the camera intrinsics and extrinsics, unscaled point clouds are reconstructed from each camera view, and a Scaled ICP method is applied to align and merge them into a unified representation. Third, point cloud–based behaviour cloning or in-context imitation learning methods are trained on the reconstructed 3D representations generated by the previous modules.

For reproducibility, full implementation details of the shared point-cloud reconstruction pipeline, including mask processing, estimator-specific depth preprocessing, calibrated back-projection, multi-view scale alignment and fusion, and policy-specific point-cloud sampling are provided in Additional Details for Point Cloud Reconstruction.

Results

1. Low-Budget Hyperparameter Screening

Exhaustively evaluating all estimators and configurations would require 216,000 rollouts, which is computationally expensive and exceeds our evaluation budget. We therefore conduct low-budget screening in simulation under the lightweight ICIL protocol. Specifically, we evaluate different combinations of the hyperparameters on 10 manipulation tasks from RLBench. For each configuration-task pair, we use five demonstrations as separate contexts and perform two Instant Policy rollouts per demonstration, yielding 10 rollouts. We average the success rates across tasks to obtain the overall screening score. Complete results are reported in Appendix I.

2. Simulation Results

2.1 In-Context Imitation Learning

For the main experiments under ICIL, we collect 20 demonstrations per task. Conditioning each demonstration as a single context, we run Instant Policy for 10 rollouts based on point clouds constructed through each depth variation. We report the average success rate across demonstrations, rollouts and tasks as the final performance metric. Note that for the depth camera baseline, we also report the all-camera setting (5 cameras), which is the same as in the original paper for fair comparison. Please refer to TABLE I in our paper for details.

2.2 Behaviour Cloning

In the BC paradigm, we use a front / side camera setup at 256 resolution and apply no image masking for any depth variation. For each task, we collect 100 demonstrations and generate depth images using each depth variant. To assess BC sensitivity to depth noise, we also add four corrupted-depth baselines by applying Gaussian noise with standard deviations of 10, 20, 30, and 40 mm to ground-truth (GT) depth. Together with the six original depth variants, this yields ten types of DP3 policies. For each policy, we evaluate up to seven checkpoints at 20-epoch intervals, using 50 rollouts with the same seeds for every checkpoint and depth variant, and select the best-performing checkpoint.

The main estimator comparisons and across-task averages are reported in Table II of our paper. Beyond these main results, the per-task visualisation below provides an additional breakdown and reveals that the effect of depth corruption varies between tasks.

Phone on Base remains at 100% success across all corruption levels, while Close Box is nearly unaffected. In contrast, Plate Out, Toilet Seat Up, and Toilet Roll Off deteriorate sharply when the noise standard deviation increases from only 10 to 20 mm. This contrast is consistent with a geometric explanation: depth noise displaces surfaces and object boundaries in the reconstructed point cloud. Tasks with clear, largely unoccluded geometry and greater action tolerance can absorb these perturbations, whereas substantial occlusion or precise contact requirements leave less spatial margin for reconstruction error.

2. Real-World Results

We evaluate the performance of different depth estimation methods on a suite of real-world manipulation tasks under challenging visual conditions, including transparent, reflective, and textureless objects. Our results show that depth estimation approaches could narrow the gap to depth images from depth cameras. Moreover, it delivers significant gains in real-world manipulation tasks under challenging visual conditions.
